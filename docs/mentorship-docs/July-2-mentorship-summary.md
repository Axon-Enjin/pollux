# UNESCO Global Youth Hackathon 2026 — Mentorship Sessions

**2 July 2026** — Three mentor sessions from the Youth Hackathon Mentorship Program, covering project design/pitching and media & information literacy (MIL).

## 1. From Ideas to Proposals: Practical Steps for Effective Project Design

**Mentor:** Dun Concha Abiera — CEO, Work Beyond Borders (founder of YABONG; Specialist Representative, UNESCO Global MIL Alliance board)

**Core message:** You don't need a perfect concept, just momentum — everyone starts messy, the pros just kept going.

- **Why pitches fail:** not because the idea is wrong, but because it isn't clear. If a listener can't repeat your project in one sentence, it's not ready. Common killers: too vague, too technical, too long, too confusing.
- **Justify it:** emotion alone isn't enough — one sharp fact turns "nice" into "necessary." Make the problem undeniable in under 10 seconds.
- **Localize it:** zoom into your street, school, or circle rather than global generalizations — smaller lens, stronger story. Speak like someone who lives it.
- **Tell the origin story:** what triggered the idea (a moment, conversation, problem you couldn't unsee), and how it moved from frustration → curiosity → action. This earns trust.
- **Shift from story to strategy:** once people are hooked, show the plan — prove the idea has legs, not just feelings.
- **Pitch structure — must-haves:**
  - What's the idea
  - What problem it solves
  - Why it matters
  - Who it helps
  - How it works
  - What you need (funds, mentors, tools, time)
  - Lead with clarity, land with impact; don't bury the gold — say the most important part first; write like you speak, cut the jargon.
- **Call to action:** you don't need to be chosen, you need to be ready. Send the application, submit the deck, ask the question — don't wait for approval, build anyway.

## 2. Digital Content Creators & Media Information Literacy

**Mentor:** Elizabeth Sherr — Ocean Science Communicator (@lizlivingblue), marine scientist turned digital storyteller/multimedia journalist

**Context on trust:** Per Pew Research (2025), 76% of 18–29 year-olds get news from social media at least sometimes, but only 50% place trust in that information. In a News Creator Corps study of 667 creators, misinformation & trust was the #1 challenge (57%), especially in Science, Race, Parenting, and Health content.

- **Real-or-fake exercises used in session:**
  - A viral photo of a diver "helping" a whale by removing barnacles is misleading — barnacle removal isn't a benign act creators should encourage.
  - The April 2025 Spain/Portugal blackout was falsely attributed to "European sanctions against Russia" via fabricated news screenshots; the real cause (per BBC) was a rare atmospheric phenomenon, and an accompanying "nuclear plant destruction" claim was actually old footage of a 2020 coal plant demolition.
- **Common dis/misinformation tactics:** using false science, cherry-picking data, spreading conspiracy theories, attacking climate solutions.
- **Tips to build confidence and trust as a creator:**
  1. Pause before you share
  2. Trace it back to its origin
  3. Look for visual tells in images/video
  4. Use reverse image search
  5. Go to the primary source for science claims
  6. Be transparent about what you don't know
- **Helpful resources:**
  - NOAA (noaa.gov) — ocean/atmosphere science, authoritative
  - iNaturalist (inaturalist.org) — wildlife ID, community science
  - AFP Fact Check (factcheck.afp.com) — multilingual, science/environment
  - EDMO (edmo.eu) — European disinformation fact-checking network (28+ countries)
  - Climate Feedback (climatefeedback.org) — science-based climate fact-checking
  - Reverse image search — Google Images, TinEye, Yandex Images
  - UNESCO's free "Navigating Climate Information with MIL" course (English/French/Spanish) — rebrand.ly/MILClimate

**Closing message:** Science-backed storytelling is one of the most powerful tools creators have.

## 3. Children, Youth and Media in 2026

**Mentor:** Sherri Hope Culver — Temple University (Professor, Media Studies & Production; Director, Certificate in Children's Media); Chair, Center for Media and Information Literacy (CMIL); Board, Global MIL Alliance; creator of *Kids Talk Media*

**Session goals:** Understand cross-platform media influence on youth → build personal opinions through research/reflection → decide where your own action can be most effective.

**Why media influence is more powerful today:** ease of access, parasocial relationships with content creators, and speed of sharing.

**Tension points raised for discussion (no fixed answers given):**
- **Influence of influencers** — figures like MrBeast and xQc shape youth beliefs about money, "normal," and success, beyond just entertainment.
- **Pop culture as propaganda** — political and state-linked messaging (e.g., pro-Kremlin propaganda via lifestyle influencer accounts) delivered through seemingly apolitical content.
- **Entertainment as propaganda** — even mainstream media/films can carry ideological messaging embedded in humor or story structure.
- **A.I. chatbots** — always-available, always-listening bots raise questions about children's motivation to connect with real humans.
- **Inspiring creativity** — if AI can create faster/easier, why should kids still bother creating? How to keep youth motivated to build real creative skills.
- **Trust in news** — constant "don't trust any source" messaging (in the name of fact-checking) risks eroding belief that journalism/democracy's "fifth estate" can be trusted at all.
- **Classroom battleground** — competing mandates (ban phones vs. integrate tech vs. screen-free zones vs. teach media literacy) leave unclear guidance for educators.
- **Politicizing MIL** — teaching youth to question/analyze media (the core of MIL) is increasingly framed by some as a "woke" agenda.
- **Social media age restrictions** — unclear if restrictions actually improve mental health/social skills, or just create a false sense of protection instead of scaffolding real media literacy over time.
- **Tech accountability** — youth are told repeatedly that educators, parents, and regulators are responsible for online safety, but rarely hear about tech/media companies changing their own algorithms or revenue models to make platforms safer.
- **Ethical awareness** — daily digital choices (sharing private images, using AI on assignments, spreading a funny disinfo meme) are fundamentally ethical questions, not just technical or rule-based ones.

**Media Literacy (NAMLE definition):** the ability to access, analyze, evaluate, create, and act using all forms of communication — one piece of the much broader Media & Information Literacy wheel (which also includes AI, data, privacy, network, and civic literacy, among ~20 other literacies).
