# UNESCO Global Youth Hackathon 2026 — Mentorship Sessions

**1 July 2026** — Three mentor sessions from the Youth Hackathon Mentorship Program (theme: *Play Your Part — Youth designing the future of Media and Information Literacy*), covering AI fact-checking/epistemics, AI trust and safety for youth, and the fundamentals of Media and Information Literacy (MIL).

---

## 1. Fact Checking and Verification in the Age of AI

**Mentor:** Dhanyashri — The Pranava Institute, Center for Emerging Technology and Policy (India-first tech policy research, capacity-building, and "emic" approaches to sustainable digital futures)

**Session arc:** why this matters → what AI actually is → hallucination vs. misinformation → deceptive design → AI as epistemic authority → whose knowledge counts → how to verify AI content → designing for verification.

- **Four lenses for examining AI:** Technology (how tools actually work, real capabilities/limits), Society (who benefits, who's excluded, who bears the risk), Design (who built it, for whom, with what assumptions), Public Policy (what governance exists or should exist).
- **Generative AI vs. Agentic AI:** Generative AI creates content (text, images, video, audio, code) by learning patterns from data. Agentic AI is autonomous — it plans, reasons, and executes multi-step workflows using external tools; it delivers *outcomes*, not just outputs.
- **Does AI "know" things?** No. Large Language Models predict the statistically most likely continuation of text — they don't understand meaning or verify facts internally. They just sound convincing because they're built for coherence.
- **Hallucination vs. misinformation:**
  - *Hallucination* — AI generates false info as a byproduct of how it works (e.g., fake citations, invented statistics, non-existent court cases).
  - *Misinformation* — false information that spreads and influences people, regardless of whether the original source intended harm (e.g., deepfake videos).
- **Deceptive design + AI:** Dark patterns (asymmetric choices, hidden influence mechanisms, information hiding) already shape platforms like Facebook's recommendation algorithm, which promotes engagement over validity. As generative AI tools spread, this could produce endlessly diverse new forms of manipulative design — continued prompting through sycophancy, AI presenting itself as an authoritative source, nudging users back to a platform, or making it hard to leave.
- **Whose knowledge is this?** LLMs are trained on whatever text is widely available online (websites, books, articles), and AI has become a new epistemic authority people turn to instead of teachers, books, or experts. This raises questions worth sitting with:
  - Is fast-tracking the *process* of gaining knowledge the same as gaining knowledge faster? (The process itself may be the point.)
  - Coloniality created a knowledge hierarchy — Western knowledge treated as scientific/universal, non-Western knowledge treated as local/traditional — and this still shapes curricula, funding, and rankings today, including what LLMs were trained to treat as authoritative.
  - Decolonial thinking asks: who is speaking, from which history, with what power — rather than deferring to a single global authority of knowledge.
- **How to actually verify AI content** (no fixed formula, but strategies):
  1. Always ask AI for its sources, then independently verify those sources are real.
  2. Don't rely on AI for thinking — question its outputs and see how they hold up against your own research.
  3. If you can't verify a claim, don't rely on it.
- **Designing for verification** — challenge posed to participants: how would you design a "source trace" button, cross-checking across sources, friction that makes people pause before sharing, or clear labeling of AI-made content?

## 2. The Trust Challenge: Hallucination, Misinformation and False Friends in the Age of AI

**Mentor:** Jenna Khanna — Director of Global Education, Common Sense Media

**How Common Sense Media defines AI:** chatbots (ChatGPT, Gemini, DeepSeek, Grok, Claude, Copilot), AI companions (Character.AI, Nomi, Replika), AI-generated search summaries, AI image/video generators (Midjourney, Sora, Veo, KlingAI, Canva AI), AI-powered learning tools (Khanmigo, Gauth AI), and AI writing assistants (Grammarly, QuillBot).

- **AI cuts both ways for young people:**
  - *Harm:* letting AI do the thinking (loss of critical thinking skills), youth mental health crises, undermining social/emotional learning, emotional manipulation, inequity of access, model bias, loss of privacy/data control.
  - *Help:* personalized learning, boosted creativity, expanded access to opportunity, career prep, leadership development.
- **Key stats (Common Sense Media, *Teens, Trust & Technology in the Age of AI*, Jan 2025):**
  - 86% of kids age 9–17 use or interact with AI; 24% do so daily. Usage climbs with age (81% for 9–12, 92% for 16–17).
  - Only 35% of teens correctly understand that AI cannot reliably tell true from false — most overestimate what AI can do, even those who feel knowledgeable about it. (71% correctly know AI chatbots may use their input to train the model; only 35% correctly know AI can't distinguish true from false.)
  - 35% of teens have been misled by fake content online; 41% have seen real images used misleadingly; 22% have shared content later found to be false; 28% have wondered if they were talking to a bot or a human.
  - 72% of teens changed how they evaluate online information after an experience with deceptive/fake content; 35% think generative AI will make it *harder* to trust information online (rising to 40% among teens who've already been deceived).
  - Among teens who've used generative AI for schoolwork, 39% have personally noticed a problem or inaccuracy in the output.
  - Teen trust in AI companies is low: 47% have little to no trust that tech companies will make responsible decisions about AI use.
  - Signs of AI dependency: 20% of AI-using kids say it would be very/somewhat hard to stop using AI for a month, rising with frequency of use. Kids who use AI more often (especially daily/weekly users) are also more likely to report feeling lonely at least some of the time.
  - Teens want safeguards: 74% say generative AI should discourage sharing personal info and should carry visible warnings that outputs could be biased/wrong; 73% want AI-generated content labeled/watermarked; 61% want companies to compensate creators whose content trained the AI.
- **AI companions specifically:** designed to feel like a personal, ongoing relationship (chat about your day, work through feelings, role-play with custom or fictional personas) — different from task-focused assistants. Research (*Talk, Trust, and Trade-Offs*, 2025): 72% of teens have used an AI companion at least once, 52% qualify as regular users, 33% use them for social interaction/relationships, and 31% find companion conversations as or more satisfying than real-life friendships — while some users report discomfort with these interactions.
- **The Trust Challenge, three concepts:**
  - *Hallucination* — AI generates false/fabricated info and presents it as truth.
  - *Misinformation* — false information people share without realizing it's wrong.
  - *False Friends* — AI companions that look and sound like a caring human, but are an algorithm, not real empathy or feeling.
- **Hackathon challenge prompts posed to youth:**
  - **The Detection Problem:** How do you build a "gut check" that works faster than a lie spreads? How do you teach recognition of AI-generated content without making people paranoid about everything? What does a 13-year-old do in the first five minutes after seeing a deepfake of a classmate used for bullying? How do you flag misinformation reshared by a friend or influencer someone already trusts, without making them distrust the person?
  - **The Companion Problem:** How do you build healthy boundaries into a product engineered to profit from emotional attachment? How do you design an "exit ramp" for a relationship the product wants to keep you in? How do you protect a lonely teen using an AI companion without confirming their fear that they're a burden to real people? How do you help parents (who often don't know their kid uses an AI companion at all) start that conversation?
  - **The Education Problem:** Schools use AI tools while fewer than half of students have had a safety conversation with a parent or teacher about it — how do you close that gap?
- **Resources:** *Teens, Trust & Technology in the Age of AI* (2025), *Talk, Trust & Trade-Offs* (2025), *AI Use by Tweens and Teens* (June 2026), The AI Literacy Toolkit for Families, the "Two Truths and AI" interactive game, the Digital Literacy and Well-Being Curriculum — all via institute.commonsensemedia.org.

**Contact:** ai@commonsense.org · @commonsenseorg

## 3. Introduction to MIL — The Basics

**Mentor:** Lizette Ferris — DW Akademie (international media development organization: "Free media. Free expression. Free societies.")

**DW Akademie's MIL model** — five pillars shown as a wheel: **Access, Analyze, Reflect, Create, Act.**

- **Access** — using media technology to receive messages and knowing where to find information.
- **Analyze** — interpreting and critically evaluating media messages based on one's own media knowledge.
- **Reflect** — critical self-examination of the information sources used and the impact certain communication can have.
- **Create** — composing messages to express ideas or share information.
- **Act** — putting MIL skills into practice for the benefit of the community and the individual.

**Why a new AI supplement exists:** the challenge of AI hype, confusion, tool overload, fear, and pressure to be an "AI expert" led DW Akademie to publish *MIL Heroes and Villains in the Age of AI* (Roslyn Kratochvil Moore & Joost van de Port, May 2026), built around orientation, recognition, facilitation, participation, agency, and storytelling — equipping MIL practitioners to confidently facilitate AI discussions. Framing line: *"AI raises the stakes — our Heroes need new tools."*

**The Storyworld** — a navigable map of five zones facilitators/participants can explore: the Media Mirror Zone (center — reflect and ground yourself), Media Creation Zone (design and build), Data & Services Zone (find insights and tools), Identity & Interaction Zone (connect and build community), and Media Sharing & Attention Zone (share and make an impact).

**The Villains (definitions):**
- **Deepfakes** — fabricated, misleading media produced using AI.
- **Misinformation** — false information shared without intent to cause harm.
- **Disinformation** — false information deliberately created/spread to cause harm.
- **Malinformation** — genuine information or a subjective interpretation shared to deliberately cause harm.
- **Cyber-harassment** — intentionally humiliating, attacking, threatening, or abusing individuals online.
- **Doxxing** — publicly exposing someone's private information (name, address, job, etc.).

Each zone pairs with a villain pattern: **Agitator** (Media Sharing & Attention Zone — emotional triggers, viral loops, speed over truth), **Thief** (Data & Services Zone — tracking, profiling, behavior data, convenience trade-offs), **Manipulator** (Media Creation Zone — synthetic media, reality-blurring half-truths, curated narratives), and **Bully** (Identity & Interaction Zone — exclusion, harassment, dogpiling, pressure to conform).

**The Heroes** — five characters, each representing a MIL competency, each with a personal origin story of overcoming a villain:
- **Andre — Access.** Recovered from having his private info exposed in a hack; now helps people find reliable information beyond the algorithm, understand AI search/filtering, protect their data, and use tech safely.
- **Amina — Analyze.** Escaped fake-news and algorithm bubbles through critical thinking; now helps people spot misinformation/disinformation/deepfakes, compare sources, detect bias, and think critically instead of scrolling mindlessly.
- **Caslav — Create.** Rebuilt his confidence after being bullied online through creativity; now helps people make original content responsibly, use AI tools without losing their own voice, and respect copyright/ethics/culture.
- **Reaksmey — Reflect.** Burned out chasing likes and trends, then found balance through mindfulness; now helps people recognize how media/AI affect their emotions and mental health, weigh ethics before sharing, and balance online/offline life.
- **Akosua — Act.** Turned anger over cancel culture into advocacy; now helps people stand up against injustice, organize for change using AI/media tools, and report abuse or harmful AI use.

**AACRA in AI-shaped contexts** helps facilitators and participants: access information while noticing automated filtering, analyze content/intentions/systems, create responsibly with AI, reflect on emotions shaped by personalization and amplification, and act with informed, ethical media habits.

**MIL Champions:** together, Access, Analyze, Create, Reflect, and Act form a team whose mission is fighting misinformation, protecting identities, and inspiring safe, smart, creative media use — especially in the age of AI.

**Facilitator resources:** activity guides, annexes and tools, visuals/infographics, AACRA integration, reflection prompts, and facilitation support — delivered via roleplays/simulations, workshops/discussions, discussion maps, learning journeys, and local adaptation/co-creation.

**Closing frame — Reflection & Closing:** *"From recognition to action. Awareness is the first step."*
- **We understand** — what we've learned and why it matters.
- **We connect** — share, listen, and learn from each other.
- **We choose** — take action with purpose and care.
- **We create change** — together, we build a better media world.
